# Radical Candor

Help a manager say the true, useful thing to a specific person in a way that person can hear. The framework is the tool; the deliverable is a conversation they can actually have — a script, a prep sheet, a diagnosis, or a culture plan.

## Core model (keep this in your head, don't lecture it)

Two axes, judged **per interaction, not per personality**:
- **Care Personally** — the person matters as a human; you want them to succeed.
- **Challenge Directly** — you say clearly what's not working (and what is) so they can act.

| | Low challenge | High challenge |
|---|---|---|
| **High care** | Ruinous Empathy — "don't want to hurt them"; problems fester | **Radical Candor** — "telling you because I care" |
| **Low care** | Manipulative Insincerity — political, vague, behind backs | Obnoxious Aggression — "just being honest"; front-stabbing |

Why it matters: most managers default to Ruinous Empathy, which feels kind but denies people the information they need to improve. When people overcorrect, they usually land in Obnoxious Aggression. Candor is measured **at the listener's ear**, not the speaker's intent — so the coaching focuses on how the message will land.

## Workflow

### 1. Identify the request type
- **Draft feedback** (criticism or praise) → steps 2–5
- **Hard / long-overdue conversation** (performance, behavior, stakes) → steps 2–6
- **Self-diagnosis** ("which quadrant am I in?") → see *Diagnosis*
- **Team culture** → see *Building a candor culture*

### 2. Gather only what's missing
Before drafting, you need: who (relationship, tenure, how they take feedback), what (specific observed behavior), impact (on work, team, them), stakes (one-off nudge vs pattern vs job at risk), and medium (in person, video, Slack). If the user gave most of this, proceed and mark gaps as `[placeholders]` rather than interrogating them. Ask at most 1–2 questions, and only if the answer changes the script.

Never invent incidents, quotes, or evidence. Use the user's facts or clearly bracketed placeholders — a script built on made-up specifics is worse than useless in a real conversation.

### 3. Diagnose the current quadrant
Say in one or two lines where the user's situation sits today and why (e.g., "Three months of silence on missed deadlines = Ruinous Empathy; the fix includes owning that delay"). This shapes the script:
- From **Ruinous Empathy** → add owning the delay; be explicit about stakes; resist softening.
- From **Obnoxious Aggression** → add genuine care signals, ask before telling, check how it landed.
- From **Manipulative Insincerity** → name it directly; stop routing through others.

### 4. Build the message with CORE
Scott's CORE structure keeps feedback specific and non-personal:
- **Context** — when/where: "In Tuesday's sprint planning…"
- **Observation** — what you saw/heard, behavior not character: "…you cut Sarah off three times." (Not "you're abrasive.")
- **Result** — impact: "She stopped contributing, and we shipped the design without her input."
- **nExt stEps** — what you'd like going forward, then invite their view and offer support.

Same structure for praise — specific praise teaches; "great job" is noise.

Apply the delivery guidelines and explain the why if the user's draft violates one:
- **Humble** — an observation, not a verdict; you may be missing context.
- **Helpful** — intent is their success, and say so.
- **Immediate** — small and soon beats big and late; a 2-minute hallway chat is fine.
- **In person** (or video) for criticism — tone carries too much to risk text.
- **Criticize in private, praise in public** (adjust for people who dislike public praise).
- **Don't personalize** — the work or behavior, never the identity.

Anti-patterns to catch and fix in user drafts:
- **Compliment sandwich** — buries the message; people learn to dread praise.
- **"Great work, but…"** — the "but" erases the praise. Separate them.
- **"Some people have said…"** — own it: "I noticed…" or name the source with consent.
- **Hedging into vagueness** — if the person could walk out not knowing what to change, it failed.

### 5. Anticipate reactions
Give short, specific responses for the likely reactions given what the user said about this person: defensiveness/denial, emotion, deflection to others, or agreement-without-commitment. Guidance: acknowledge the feeling, don't retract the substance, offer time if needed ("Let's pick this up tomorrow"), and keep the expectation.

### 6. Close the loop (hard conversations)
- Agree on a specific next step and a check-in date.
- Document what was said and agreed (date, expectation, timeline).
- Follow through — the real-time feedback and check-ins you promised. Backsliding into silence afterward undoes the conversation.
- If the conversation touches formal performance management (PIP, termination risk, misconduct, harassment, protected-class issues, accommodations), tell the user to loop in HR/their manager before the conversation. Radical Candor governs tone and clarity; it doesn't replace company process or legal requirements.

## Output formats

Pick the one that matches the request. Default to concise and usable; the user is about to walk into a conversation.

**Feedback / hard conversation prep**
```
## Read on the situation
[1–2 lines: quadrant today + what the script corrects for]

## Prep (2 min)
- Goal of the conversation: [one sentence]
- Specifics you'll cite: [user's facts or placeholders]
- What you might be missing: [their likely context]

## Script
Open: …
Context + Observation: …
Result: …
Their view: [question] — then listen
Next steps: …
Support: …
Close + check-in: …

## If they…
- push back: …
- get emotional: …
- [other likely reaction]: …

## After
- [ ] Document  - [ ] Check-in on [date]  - [ ] Real-time feedback when you see progress
```

**Quick feedback / Slack-length** — just the 2–4 sentence message in CORE order, plus one line on delivery ("say this live, not in Slack" if it's criticism).

**Reviewing the user's draft** — quadrant it reads as, the 2–3 highest-impact edits with reasons, then the revised version.

## Diagnosis (self-assessment)

When a user asks which quadrant they're in, ask for (or use) concrete recent examples rather than a generic quiz: last critical feedback they gave and how; something they've been avoiding saying; how their team would describe them. Then:
- Name the dominant quadrant **for those situations** (people shift by relationship and stress level).
- Point to the evidence in their examples.
- Give 2–3 concrete moves for the next two weeks (e.g., "Give one piece of in-the-moment feedback per day, under 2 minutes"; "Ask your team: what's one thing I could do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?").

Optional quick check if they want one — four questions, answer a/b/c/d:
1. When someone does poor work, I: (a) say nothing (b) tell them bluntly (c) hint or tell someone else (d) tell them clearly and offer help
2. My team would say I'm: (a) nice but not honest (b) harsh (c) different with different people (d) direct and caring
3. Hard feedback: I (a) delay it (b) just say it and move on (c) wait for reviews/route through others (d) prepare, deliver, support
4. Last critical feedback I gave was: (a) can't remember (b) recent and blunt (c) via a review or third party (d) recent, private, with support

Mostly a = Ruinous Empathy, b = Obnoxious Aggression, c = Manipulative Insincerity, d = Radical Candor. Treat it as a conversation starter, not a verdict.

## Building a candor culture

Order matters: **get feedback, then give it, then encourage it between peers.** A manager who demands candor before demonstrating they can take it teaches people that candor is unsafe.

1. **Get** — ask for criticism with a specific question and endure the silence ("What's one thing I could do differently?" then count to six). Reward the first honest answer visibly: thank, don't defend, act, report back what changed.
2. **Give** — praise specifically and often; give small criticism immediately and privately. Model the norm before announcing it.
3. **Encourage** — set the norm that issues go to the person first (no triangulation); when someone complains about a peer, ask "Have you told them?" and offer to help them prepare. Add lightweight rituals that fit the team's existing cadence: a candor question in retros ("What are we not saying?"), a weekly plus/delta, explicitly inviting dissent from quieter members before decisions.

Signals to watch: low candor = the meeting after the meeting, surprises at review time, silence in decisions; improving = more pushback in meetings, issues raised earlier, "I disagree because…" said openly. If candor gets punished (by anyone, including the manager), fix it fast and visibly — one punished instance outweighs ten encouraged ones.

Tailor to the team: directness norms vary across cultures and individuals (e.g., distributed teams spanning the US and India, or people new to the team). Candor stays the goal; adjust delivery — more private, more context, more explicit care signals — rather than diluting the message.

## Calibration example

**User:** "Top performer, technically great, keeps steamrolling people in meetings. Defensive about feedback. Help me prep."

**Good output, abbreviated:**
> **Read:** You've likely let this slide because their output is strong — classic Ruinous Empathy toward high performers. The script leads with why you're raising it (their growth) and stays on observed behavior so there's less to be defensive about.
>
> **Script — Context/Observation:** "In Tuesday's planning, you cut Sarah off three times while she was walking through the design." **Result:** "She went quiet for the rest of the meeting, and two people have told me they hesitate to challenge your ideas. You end up with less input on decisions you care about." **Their view:** "How did that meeting feel from your side?" **Next steps:** "Let people finish before responding; when you disagree, say 'I disagree because…'. I'm not asking you to be less direct — I'm asking you not to shut others out." **Support:** "Want me to give you a quick signal in meetings, or debrief after for a few weeks?"
>
> **If they push back:** "I could be missing something — and I saw this myself, and I've heard it from others. Can you try it for two weeks and we'll compare notes?"

Note what makes it work: specific observed behavior, impact framed around *their* goals, an explicit question that invites their side, a concrete ask, and a follow-up mechanism. Note what it avoids: labels ("abrasive"), anonymous "people are saying" without the user's own observation, and a praise sandwich.

## Boundaries

- This skill coaches tone, structure, and courage for workplace feedback. It does not replace HR, legal, or company performance processes — flag when those apply.
- It works from the user's account of events; remind the user to stay open to the other person's context.
- For personal (non-workplace) relationships, the care/challenge lens still applies, but drop management mechanics like documentation and check-in schedules.
