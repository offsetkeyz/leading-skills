# Worked examples

These show the expected depth and shape of output: concrete, tailored, and naming the Grove principle behind each move.

---

## Example 1: Rebuilding a manager's week around leverage

**Input:** "I manage 8 backend engineers. I'm in meetings about 30 hours a week, I still review most PRs, and I feel like I'm not adding value."

**Output (abridged):**

### Diagnosis

- **Span of control:** 8 direct reports is at the top of Grove's 6–8 range for a manager whose main job is supervising. You're at capacity, so the lever is leverage, not more hours.
- **Negative leverage spotted:**
  - **Reviewing most PRs is meddling.** It makes you the limiting step and teaches the team to wait for you.
  - **Status meetings recite information that could be read**, which is low leverage.

### Changes

| Change | Grove principle |
|---|---|
| Set 1:1 cadence by TRM: weekly for the 2 newest engineers, biweekly for the 4 mid-level, every 3 weeks for the 2 high-TRM seniors (with an open door) | 1:1 frequency follows TRM |
| Replace the daily status meeting with an async update plus a 5-indicator dashboard reviewed in the staff meeting | Indicators over recitation |
| Stop routine PR review. Name two seniors as review owners, and sample 2–3 PRs a week, weighted toward the low-TRM engineers and high-risk areas | Monitor by sampling; delegation isn't abdication |
| Hold "decision hours" twice a week for anything people are blocked on you for | Delayed decisions are negative leverage |
| Cluster 1:1s on Tue/Thu mornings and protect two 3-hour focus blocks for planning and the hiring pipeline | Batching; calendar as production schedule |

### Expected rhythm

Roughly 14 hours in meetings, 6 hours of sampling and decisions, and 10+ hours of protected high-leverage work.

### What you decide

Which seniors own review, and which areas count as high-risk enough that you keep sampling them more often.

## Example 2: A new tech lead (TRM)

**Input:** "A 5-year engineer just became tech lead. How do I manage them?"

**Output (abridged):**

### Assessment

High TRM at engineering, **low TRM at leadership**. Manage the leadership work accordingly, and avoid directive treatment of their technical work.

### Phases

| Phase | Style | 1:1s | Monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days 0–30 | Structured | Weekly plus short check-ins | Review their sprint plans and first difficult feedback conversation *before* they happen (lowest-value stage) |
| Days 30–90 | Individual-oriented | Weekly | Debrief after key events: "what did you try, and what would you change?" |
| Day 90+ | Minimal involvement | Biweekly | Agree on objectives and watch team indicators; sample occasionally |

### Decision checklist

For the first month, list the decisions they check with you on before acting, such as scope changes, staffing moves, and pushback to other teams. Shrink the list on a set date. Say why up front: "This isn't about trust. It's so you learn the judgment behind these calls."

### Warning signs

- **They revert to IC work.** That's meddling on their own team.
- **The team says direction is unclear.**
- **Decisions pile up waiting on them.**

### The transition conversation

> "You've been one of our strongest engineers, and this is a new job with new skills. For the first month I'll be more hands-on with the leadership side. That's not about your engineering, which I won't second-guess. As you get comfortable I'll step back, and the goal is that you're running this independently in about 90 days. What are you most uncertain about?"
