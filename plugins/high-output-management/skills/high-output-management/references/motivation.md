# Motivation (Grove)

## Can't or won't

When someone isn't performing, Grove gives two possible reasons: **they can't** (they lack the skill or knowledge) or **they won't** (they lack motivation). These need different fixes, and treating one as the other wastes time and damages trust.

- **Can't:** a training or TRM problem. Increase structure, coach, pair them with someone, or narrow the scope.
- **Won't:** a motivation problem. Read on.

Diagnose before acting: ask, observe, and check whether the person has ever succeeded at this kind of task.

## What motivation is

Grove's view: a manager can't directly motivate someone. The job is to **create an environment in which motivated people can flourish**. Motivation shows up as performance, not as satisfaction, so a comfortable but unproductive team is not a motivated team.

He uses Maslow's hierarchy to explain what a person is currently responding to:

| Need | At work | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Physiological | Pay that covers basics | Rarely the live issue for engineers |
| Safety / security | Job stability, a predictable environment | Spikes during layoffs and reorgs. Address it directly. |
| Social / belonging | Being part of a team that works | Isolation, especially remote, erodes it |
| Esteem / recognition | Peer respect, visible credit | Specific, timely recognition matters more than the size of a reward |
| Self-actualization | Competence and achievement | The only need that is open-ended, and so the one that sustains high output |

**Key points:**

- **A satisfied need stops motivating.** Once someone feels secure, more security does nothing; the next need up takes over.
- **Money's role changes with the need.** At lower levels money is the need itself. Higher up, it's a scorecard of esteem, so *relative* fairness matters more than the absolute amount.
- **Self-actualization comes in two flavors.** Some people are driven by competence (mastery of the craft) and some by achievement (hitting a stretch goal, especially one around 50% likely to succeed). Engineers are often competence-driven, so give them hard problems and room to master them.
- **Fear of failure motivates only in moderation.** Too much of it paralyzes people.

## The manager as coach

Grove's sports analogy: the best managers act like coaches.

- **They take no personal credit** for the team's wins, which builds trust.
- **They're tough on the team** in order to get its best performance, by setting a high bar and giving honest feedback.
- **They have played the game,** so they understand the work and can teach it.

Output becomes visible when it's measured, and measurement plus feedback supports both competence drive and achievement drive. That's why indicators and OKRs (see `indicators.md` and `okrs.md`) are motivation tools as well as control tools.

## Applying it

When a user describes a disengaged or underperforming report:

1. **Can't or won't?** State which it looks like and what evidence would confirm it.
2. **If won't:** which need is likely unmet? Look for recent changes such as a reorg, losing a project, a peer getting promoted, or isolation.
3. **Prescribe environmental changes, not pep talks.** Examples: restore security with clear information, rebuild belonging by pairing or tightening the team, give specific recognition, or offer a stretch problem with visible stakes.
4. **Check the TRM fit.** Boredom often means TRM has outgrown the management style or the scope.
5. **Define the indicator** that will show whether things are improving, and review it in 1:1s.
